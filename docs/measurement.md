# Acoustic Measurement

Measure frequency response, impulse response, distortion, and decay, and create correction EQ when needed:
1. Launch [Acoustic Measurement](https://effetune.frieve.com/features/measurement/measurement.html), or select **Acoustic Measurement** from the Settings menu.
2. Follow the guided setup to configure your measurement microphone and output device.
3. Measure your system's frequency response at one or more listening positions.
4. Generate a parametric EQ correction that can be directly imported into EffeTune.
5. Apply the correction to achieve a more accurate, neutral sound reproduction.

## Output Channels

Set **Output Device Channels** to match your output device: **6** for 5.1 or **8** for 7.1. Output channel and per-channel bandwidth choices stay within this range. Test signals and measurements always use this channel count, so keep **6** even when measuring only Ch 3 (center) and Ch 4 (subwoofer) on a 5.1 device.

For a multichannel system, select **All Channels** to measure all outputs together, or select individual **Output Channel** entries to measure them one at a time. Under **Advanced Settings**, choose **Off**, **Same for All Channels**, or **Per Channel** for sweep bandwidth. With **Per Channel**, use **Channel to Configure** to set each selected output channel's frequency range. During level adjustment, **Channel Mode** starts at **Automatic Rotation**; select a specific test-signal channel or **Manual** when needed.

## Importing an Impulse Response

If you already have an impulse-response WAV file, choose **Import** and select it. EffeTune saves each WAV channel as a measurement result that can be used in **Room EQ** and other measurement-based features.

## Audio Interface Calibration

To remove your audio interface's own response, connect its output directly to its input and save that loopback as a normal, uncalibrated measurement with an impulse response. For your speaker measurement, select that saved point under **Audio Interface Calibration** while keeping the same interface, channels, sample rate, and input/output gain settings. Choose **None (uncalibrated)** to measure without loopback calibration. Only measurements made without the Effect Pipeline can be used as a calibration source.

## Measuring with the Effect Pipeline

To check by measurement what your correction actually does in the room, open Acoustic Measurement from the main EffeTune app and set **Effect Pipeline** to **Current pipeline**. The test signal then passes through your current Effect Pipeline (Room EQ, Channel Divider, FIR Crossover, XTC, and so on) before it is played, so the microphone captures the corrected system. Leave it at **None** to measure the speakers alone. The setting returns to **None** each time you start a new measurement, so select **Current pipeline** again whenever you want it.

- **When it is available:** The pipeline is handed over only when you open Acoustic Measurement from the main app. If you start it directly or reload the page, only **None** can be selected; reopen it from the main app while audio processing is running.
- **Channel means pipeline input:** With **Current pipeline** selected, the channel field becomes **Pipeline Input Channel**; choose the pipeline input channel that receives the test signal. The pipeline's outputs are played on all of the device's channels, so crossovers, upmixing, and XTC sound as they do in normal listening. This also applies to the noise used for level adjustment. Per-channel sweep bandwidth and per-channel interface calibration are applied per pipeline input channel.
- **Fixed settings:** **Sampling Frequency** and **Output Device Channels** follow the main app and cannot be changed. If the device cannot run at that sample rate or has fewer channels, the measurement stops with a message.
- **Level:** The test signal level is the level at the pipeline input, the same as **Level** in the [Pipeline Analyzer](pipeline-analyzer.md). If the processed signal would exceed full scale, for example because of EQ boost, the measurement stops before playback; lower the level and try again.
- **Nonlinear and time-varying effects:** As in the Pipeline Analyzer, the result is a snapshot at the current settings and level. Compressors and saturators depend on the level, and modulated or random effects keep changing during the repeated sweeps.
- **Results:** Measurements made this way are marked **Pipeline** in the list, and **Measurement Details** shows **Effect Pipeline** as **Applied** (with the plugin names when available). They are already corrected, so they cannot be used for correction EQ design, copying or export, Room EQ, XTC, speaker responses in the Pipeline Analyzer, or as an interface calibration source. Their frequency response and other graphs are shown as usual.
- **Not on Android:** Android cannot measure through the Effect Pipeline.

## Measuring Speaker Distortion

This measurement finds out how each of your speakers distorts at your listening volume and prepares the [Distortion Correction](plugins/others.md#distortion-correction) effect. Choose **+ New Measurement**, then **Measure speaker distortion** under **Speaker distortion**. It needs a sample rate of 44.1 kHz or higher. Many speakers cannot be improved by this correction; even then, the result shows what kind of distortion they have.

**Setup**
- **Channels to measure:** Each checked channel is measured on its own, one after another. Below it, **Sweep Bandwidth** limits the test signals; it is the same setting as under **Advanced Settings** of the frequency response measurement (see [Output Channels](#output-channels)). For a full-range speaker, leave it at **Off**. When each amplifier channel drives its own driver through a crossover, choose **Per Channel** and set each channel to the range its driver plays. A correction measured with a limited band is meant for a channel that plays only that range; a note below the setting shows the band of each limited channel.
- **Amplifier volume:** Set the amplifier to the loudest volume you listen at and leave it there; the correction is made for this volume. Afterwards, change loudness inside EffeTune. If you later turn the amplifier down, use **Check again** (below); if you turn it up beyond this volume, measure again.
- **Microphone position:** Choose **In front of the speaker (recommended)**. Place the microphone 0.5–1 m (20–40 in) in front of the speaker being measured, on its axis and pointing at it, at the height midway between the woofer and the tweeter (at the centre of a single-driver speaker), away from table tops and walls. In a room with a lot of echo, use 0.5 m. Before each channel, the app asks you to move the microphone in front of that speaker. This is different from the listening-position measurements used for Room EQ. **Listening position** saves moving the microphone but usually corrects less.
- **Distortion Averaging:** More averaging lowers the noise in the result but takes longer; the time per channel is shown beside it. **Quick** is for rooms that cannot be kept quiet for long: it takes about half the time of **×2**. At the default **Signal Level**, it removes about as much distortion as **×2** in a quiet room, and up to about 2 dB less with more background noise. With a lower **Signal Level** in a noisy room, it is more likely to leave the correction off.
- **Signal Level:** How loud the test signals are. Short loud peaks play 10 dB higher, so at −10 dBFS they reach full scale. Lower it if the level check sounded strained; the correction still applies at your listening volume.
- **Retry on Noise** is **On** by default. It replays a multitone test signal up to two more times if its recording is too noisy or unusable, using the noise measured during the channel’s level check as the initial reference. If no take passes the noise check, the app keeps the quietest usable recording, if there is one, and uses its noise level as the new reference. Sweeps, silence recordings, and **Check again** run without automatic retries.

**What you will hear.** **Run level check** first plays about 5 seconds of the test signals, including the loudest peak, and checks that the microphone neither overloads nor hears the signal too faintly; the **Input Level** meter shows what the microphone picks up the whole time this step is open. **Start Measurement** then plays, per channel, a short level check, a series of music-like test signals with short loud peaks, and a correction check of about a minute that plays the test signal without and with the correction. When the correction of low-frequency odd-order distortion looks useful, about 20 more seconds play at high volume to confirm it does no harm there; this is the loudest sustained sound of the measurement. **Before you start** shows the total time; while the measurement runs, a bar under each channel shows how far it has got. If noise keeps getting into a channel, click **Measure … again** next to **Cancel** and confirm. This works with **Retry on Noise** on or off. The test signals recorded so far for that channel are discarded and it starts again from its first test signal, without a new level check. Finished channels are kept. If the microphone input overloads during the distortion test signals, that channel stops within about a second on desktop and web. On Android, it stops after the current test signal finishes. The message tells you how many dB to lower the input by. Lower it, then click **Measure … again** to repeat the channel from its level check.

The test signals are loud. Let people nearby know or ask them to leave the room, consider ear protection if you stay close to the speaker, and keep your neighbours in mind. If you hear crackling, scraping or a hard knock from the speaker, press **Cancel** immediately and confirm with **Cancel measurement**; the test signals keep playing until you confirm, and nothing is kept.

For an accurate result, the microphone should hear nothing but the test signal, from exactly the same spot the whole time:
- **Keep the room as silent as you can.** Voices, footsteps, fans, air conditioning and noise from outside all make the result less accurate. Turn off what you can, close doors and windows, and stay still and quiet until the measurement finishes.
- **Fix the microphone completely.** Mount it firmly on a stand so that it cannot move at all; do not hold it in your hand, and do not touch it, its stand or its cable while a channel is measured.
- **Give the microphone enough signal.** The level check result shows the signal-to-noise ratio: how far the test signal stands above the noise between 50 Hz and 8 kHz. Correcting distortion of 1 % needs at least 40 dB. Below that, a warning tells you how far you can raise the microphone gain; if there is no room left, make the room quieter. You can still start the measurement, but the correction will be less accurate.

**Reading the result.** The **Distortion** tab shows one block per channel with **Correction on** or **Correction off** and a short reason. A bar chart compares the total, even-order and odd-order distortion from 50 Hz to 8 kHz **Without the correction** and **With the correction**, in dB and %; the legend says whether the corrected figures were measured in the correction check or predicted, and a corrected value in the warning colour means that distortion is higher with the correction. All channels share the same scale. If a channel was measured louder than the amplifier or the speaker can play, a warning at the top of the tab says the volume is too high: turn down the amplifier volume or lower the **Signal Level** and measure again, because the distortion at this limit cannot be corrected and the correction works only at volumes below it. **Correction off** is a common, normal result: the correction stays off when the distortion it removes was too small to measure, or when it would reduce distortion only slightly, make part of it worse, or add distortion at lower volume, on loud peaks, or to single tones. The main correction reduces second-order (even-order) distortion; the **Odd-order** line says whether low-frequency odd-order distortion is also corrected on that channel, or why not. The **Recommended Filter Length** line names the length that corrects the channels best. For a channel left off, **See what the measurement found** opens the [Diagnosis](#reading-the-distortion-diagnosis) tab for it. Enter a name and click **Save**. In the measurement list, saved results are marked **Distortion** and show a small graph of the even-order distortion before correction.

**Details.** Click **Details** to open it for all channels at once. The first chart shows the change compared with no correction for each Filter Length (**Short** and **Long**): a bar to the left of 0 dB means less distortion, a bar to the right means more. It lists the even-order distortion in several frequency ranges and the total distortion at the set **Signal Level**, then the even-order distortion predicted at a 6 dB and 12 dB lower **Signal Level** and on loud peaks. When odd-order correction was measured, a second chart shows how much it changes the odd-order and total distortion compared with the same setting without it.

**Check the correction.** After turning the amplifier down, click **Check again** with the microphone placed as in the measurement. It plays the test signal for about 30–40 seconds per channel, measures how much the amplifier volume changed, sets **Amplifier Volume Change** in the effect, and measures how well the correction works now. If the amplifier is louder than in the measurement, measure again at this volume.

**Upper Limit.** Under **Correction settings**, **Upper Limit (Hz)** sets the highest frequency that is corrected (500–8000 Hz); lower it to correct only the bass and midrange. **Update result** reuses the recordings, so nothing is played, but the measured figures become predictions until you run **Check again**.

**Using the result.** Add Distortion Correction at the end of the Effect Pipeline and choose the saved measurement in its **Measurement** list. Or click **Copy Distortion Correction settings**, click an empty area of the Effect Pipeline and paste (Ctrl+V).

## Reading and Exporting Results

Results of a frequency-response measurement have five tabs; results of a speaker distortion measurement have six: **Distortion**, **Diagnosis**, **Single Tones**, **Harmonics**, **Level & Noise**, and **Measurement Details**. Frequency averages can include several points or outputs; the impulse, distortion, and waterfall tabs select one saved point and output. When switching from an average, the last valid individual selection is restored, or the first available record is selected. Returning to frequency response restores its selection. A measurement with all outputs playing together describes their combined response.

- **Frequency Response** compares the measured response, correction EQ, and predicted corrected response. Set the frequency and dB ranges, display smoothing, relative normalization or reference level, and visible curves. Smoothing uses a Gaussian width (σ); **0** adds no smoothing but cannot undo processing already present in the saved response. For an individual record with an impulse response, choose the saved response or reanalyze the IR using the selected window before/after onset and window shape. The results may differ because they use different time intervals. Display settings do not change the saved measurement or correction EQ. Correction design, copying, and TXT/CSV export remain in the correction section.
- **Impulse Response** shows signed amplitude or the dB magnitude of the response over time. Adjust the time and amplitude ranges, zoom/pan, peak normalization, displayed time origin, and window lengths and shape. The overlay identifies the analyzed interval. Time zero starts at the detected onset; moving it changes the display, not the measured travel time. A WAV import or a recording without a playback timing reference does not provide absolute propagation delay. WAV export saves the complete stored, unnormalized linear response as mono 32-bit float audio at its saved sample rate, regardless of the displayed window or zoom. Use the mouse wheel over the graph to zoom the time axis around the point under the cursor.
- **Distortion** shows harmonic ratios relative to the fundamental, initially H2, H3, and THD. In **Display settings**, the **Highest displayed harmonic** slider selects up to H10 when the measurement contains those orders; a record limited to H3 needs a new measurement for higher orders. **THD highest order** separately sets the THD ceiling and cannot exceed the display limit. Adjust frequency and vertical ranges, visible curves, and the analysis window. Under **Unit**, choose % or dBr; both express the same ratio to the fundamental (1% = −40 dBr). A larger ratio means more harmonic distortion. THD combines orders 2 through its ceiling and appears only where all are valid; gaps mean unavailable data, not zero distortion. For distortion, **Hann** gives full weight to the direct-response onset and tapers to zero at the selected edges; changing the window can change the curves. Higher harmonics have shorter usable windows and narrower frequency coverage; a longer requested sweep can improve separation. Results describe the complete playback and recording path; linear interface calibration does not remove its distortion. For a speaker distortion measurement, this tab instead shows the correction result described in [Measuring Speaker Distortion](#measuring-speaker-distortion).
- **Diagnosis** explains, for one channel of a speaker distortion measurement, what kind of distortion was found and what can be done about it; **Single Tones**, **Harmonics**, and **Level & Noise** show the charts behind it. See [Reading the Distortion Diagnosis](#reading-the-distortion-diagnosis).
- **Waterfall (3D)** offers **Waterfall** and **Burst Decay** analysis. Surface height and heatmap color show the same relative dB level; the color scale and position readout identify the level. Contour lines connect points at the same dB level. Frequency grid lines follow the surface at the axis ticks. Under **Normalization**, **None** keeps the original levels. **Overall peak = 0 dB** uses one peak across the displayed frequency band and available times, preserving level differences. **Each frequency peak = 0 dB** sets each frequency’s loudest available time to 0 dB, making decay easier to compare; that peak need not occur at 0 ms. The default view places zero depth at the back and later values toward the front. Set the frequency and dB ranges and first/last slices. Adjust rotation, elevation and zoom with the view controls, or drag to rotate and use the wheel or + / − to zoom. **Reset view** restores the viewpoint without changing the analysis. In **Waterfall**, depth is time in ms; adjust window length, rise time, window shape and smoothing. Longer windows distinguish nearby frequencies better but blur changes in time; the rise interval also affects time resolution. Only complete windows within the saved response are shown. **Burst Decay** shows the envelope of Gaussian-shaped tone bursts with a fixed 1/3-octave bandwidth. Choose **Time** in ms or **Cycles** for depth; the initial cycle range is 0–30. Cycles help compare ringing in periods: one cycle is 10 ms at 100 Hz and 1 ms at 1 kHz, so equal cycle depths are different elapsed times. The burst duration itself contributes to the displayed decay. This estimate assumes silence before the first saved sample while retaining saved samples before onset; gaps indicate insufficient recorded tail, not silence.
- **Measurement Details** shows acquisition, calibration, sample rate, timing reference, saved duration, and available analyses. Export JSON here; including impulse responses also includes available harmonic data. A JSON export without impulse responses retains frequency results but cannot restore time or distortion analyses.

Use the graph legends and position readouts to inspect values with the mouse, touch, or keyboard. In **Frequency Response** and **Distortion**, hover over a legend entry to highlight its curve. Automatic range and reset controls help return to a useful view. These are relative levels, not calibrated sound-pressure levels. Frequency readouts also show λ, the length of one wavelength in meters, calculated for dry air at 15°C and 1 atm (sound speed 340.3 m/s).

Use the camera icon (**Copy image**) above any of the four graphs to copy a high-resolution PNG to the clipboard, then paste it into a presentation or blog editor. The image keeps the current view and theme, title, selected measurement, legend and color scale where shown, without the cursor or settings controls.

New measurements use an exponential sine sweep (ESS). Start with a low output level and adjust it without clipping. Distortion needs valid harmonic data from this measurement; imported WAV responses and measurements containing only frequency or linear IR data cannot supply it. If data is unavailable, import a measurement containing the needed responses or make a new measurement. The available range also depends on the sweep band and recorded duration. The requested sweep length may be increased to separate harmonics. Each repeat also includes 2.5 seconds for decay. With bandwidth limiting off, ESS covers 20 Hz–20 kHz within the limits of the actual sample rate.

To simulate the active pipeline's frequency, phase, group delay, impulse response, distortion, and decay—including up to four selected outputs and optional saved speaker responses—see the [Pipeline Analyzer guide](pipeline-analyzer.md). Use [Measuring with the Effect Pipeline](#measuring-with-the-effect-pipeline) to confirm the result with a microphone.

### Reading the Distortion Diagnosis

The **Diagnosis**, **Single Tones**, **Harmonics**, and **Level & Noise** tabs show, for one channel of a [speaker distortion measurement](#measuring-speaker-distortion), what kind of distortion your speaker has, whether the correction can reduce it, and how it compares with another measurement. They only display the saved results; they play nothing and change nothing. Choose the channel under **Channel**; the four tabs share it, so changing it on one tab changes it on all. **Compare with** is on the **Diagnosis** tab. **Band resolution** sits in the three charts it changes (**Distortion at each Signal Level**, **Harmonic distortion**, and **Noise during playback**) and is one setting for all of them: wider bands can show smaller distortion; narrower bands show where a narrow problem sits. Values are relative to the test signal (in **Harmonic distortion** and **Harmonic orders**, to the fundamental), not calibrated sound-pressure levels. The charts are shown one per row, and the camera icon (**Copy image**) at the top right of each chart copies it as a PNG image, like the graphs of the other tabs.

Each chart can show its own range. **Auto range**, at the top right of the chart next to **Copy image**, fits the range to the curves shown, and **Reset** returns it to the default; **Display settings** under the chart sets **Frequency from**, **Frequency to**, **Level from**, and **Level to** directly. Levels use the unit of the chart's vertical axis (percent or dB); on the color maps they set the range the colors cover. For a very clean device whose distortion lies below the default range, for example, lower **Level from**. The ranges are kept while the window is open, also when you change the channel, tab, or measurement, but are not saved; the readout under the pointer and **Copy image** follow the range shown.

The line under the controls lists the channel, date, microphone position, Signal Level and loud-peak level, and Distortion Averaging. It ends with where the "with the correction" figures come from: **Checked right after the measurement** (the correction check), **Checked again** with the date of the last **Check again**, or **Not checked since the Upper Limit changed**, in which case they are predictions until you run **Check again**. It also warns when the microphone may have distorted.

#### Diagnosis

**What the measurement found** lists the patterns found in the charts, each with a likely cause and what you can do, tagged **correctable** when the Distortion Correction effect can reduce it and **not correctable** otherwise. The causes are likely explanations, not a certain diagnosis. When the chart behind a finding is on another tab, **See the … tab** at its end opens that tab. **Volume too high** comes first when it applies; measure again at a lower volume, as the warning on the **Distortion** tab says. When a rattle is found, **Correct only below … (avoids the rattle)** sets the **Upper Limit** below it and updates the result without playing anything.

- **Distortion at each Signal Level**: Distortion by frequency, with one solid line for each measured Signal Level (the set level, 6 dB and 12 dB lower, and loud peaks); lower is better. As its axis title **Multitone distortion (dB vs. test signal)** says, this is distortion measured with a music-like signal of many simultaneous tones, relative to the test signal, so the values cannot be compared directly with THD figures measured with a single tone (the **Harmonics** tab shows those). Choose **Even-order** or **Odd-order**; of the odd-order distortion, the correction reduces only low-frequency odd-order distortion below 2 kHz, and only when the correction check measured a benefit. Distortion that drops by about 6 dB at each 6 dB lower level behaves like second-order distortion, the kind the correction reduces; lines that stay together do not depend on level. The dashed line was measured with the correction in the correction check, and dotted lines are predicted with the correction; they are drawn even for a channel whose correction is off, so you can see why. **Short** and **Long** choose which Filter Length's result is shown (the recommended one is marked **(recommended)**); this changes only the display. The grey area is **Below the noise floor**: distortion there is hidden under the noise, so the correction in that range is estimated from the range the measurement can see, and the correction check cannot confirm its effect there. A quieter room or a higher **Distortion Averaging** makes this area smaller. The vertical line marks the **Upper Limit**.

**Summary** lists the total distortion in four frequency ranges at the set Signal Level and 6 dB and 12 dB lower, the change at 6 dB lower, and the **Likely kind** of distortion in each range. The lines below the table give the 50 Hz–8 kHz totals before → after the correction for the Filter Length shown in the chart above, marked as measured in the correction check or predicted, plus the predicted even-order figures at 6 dB lower Signal Level and on loud peaks. **Copy summary as text** copies the table for a spreadsheet.

**Compare with** adds another channel of this measurement, or a channel of another speaker distortion measurement on this device, as dashed lines in **Distortion at each Signal Level** and an extra line in each summary cell that says whether this measurement is cleaner, about the same, or has more distortion. It does not change the other tabs. When both used the same microphone and placement, the comparison is matched by loudness at the microphone; keep the microphone gain and distance the same for a fair comparison. Otherwise values are compared at the same Signal Level only.

#### Single Tones

- **2nd harmonic of single tones**: The 2nd harmonic of a single tone at each fundamental frequency, at the set Signal Level; lower is better. The solid line was measured without the correction, and the dotted lines show what to expect with the correction for **Short** and **Long**. Where a dotted line is above the solid line, the correction would add distortion to single tones; when it would do so across a frequency range, the correction stays off. The grey area is below the noise floor.
- **3rd harmonic of single tones**: The same for the 3rd harmonic, with one dotted line for the odd-order correction. It is drawn whenever an odd-order correction was fitted, also when that correction stays off. Where it would add distortion to single tones across a frequency range, the odd-order correction stays off.
- **Static distortion at the wave peaks**: The part of the 2nd and 3rd harmonic distortion that acts like a fixed input–output curve, shown as the gain at the positive and the negative peak of a single tone at the set Signal Level, compared with a quiet tone, for fundamentals up to 3.9 kHz. Below 0 dB the peak is squashed, above 0 dB it is stretched. Lines that split apart mean the two halves of the wave are treated differently, which creates even-order distortion such as the 2nd harmonic; lines that move together mean symmetric, odd-order distortion such as the 3rd harmonic. The dotted lines show where the solid lines would be if all of the 2nd and 3rd harmonic distortion were of this kind; where the solid lines stay much closer to 0 dB than the dotted lines, most of the distortion at that frequency changes with frequency or timing, for example near the speaker's resonance, and does not appear in this chart. The grey area is below the noise floor. **View** switches between **Peaks**, the lines described above, and **Map**, which shows the whole wave rather than only its peaks: each column is a fundamental frequency, the top and bottom edges are the positive and the negative peak (the two solid lines of **Peaks**), and the color shows the gain at each instantaneous level in between, blue where the wave is squashed and orange where it is stretched. The levels between the peaks are calculated from the 2nd and 3rd harmonics, not measured, and a column without color has no static distortion above the noise floor. The vertical range of **Peaks** and the color scale of **Map** adjust themselves to the largest gain change found, so small distortion stays readable; read the numbers on the axis or the color bar before comparing two measurements or channels.

#### Harmonics

- **Harmonic distortion**: The usual THD (total harmonic distortion, the 2nd to the 10th harmonic) of the speaker without the correction, by fundamental frequency at the set Signal Level, in percent of the fundamental, with the 2nd, 3rd, and 5th harmonics (H2, H3, H5); lower is better. It is measured with sine sweeps, like the THD figures of single-tone measurements. The thin dashed line is THD at 6 dB lower Signal Level; where it is well below the solid THD line, the distortion grows with level.
- **Harmonic orders**: A color map of each harmonic from the 2nd to the 10th by fundamental frequency, at the set Signal Level, in dB relative to the fundamental (dBr). Brighter cells are stronger harmonics.

#### Level & Noise

- **Harmonic change at 6 dB lower Signal Level**: How much H2, H3, and H5 change when the test signal is 6 dB lower. Distortion near the −6 dB guide (**Typical of correctable distortion**) is of the kind this correction can reduce; distortion that drops much more is not. When the volume was too high, the affected range is shaded and marked **Volume too high**; distortion there that drops far more than the guide comes from the sound being cut off at the limit and cannot be corrected, so lower the volume and measure again.
- **Gain change with Signal Level**: The speaker's gain at the set Signal Level, compared with 6 dB lower. A line at 0 dB means the output follows the level; a dip, typically in the bass, shows that the speaker or the amplifier is compressing at the set level. A shaded range marked **Volume too high** shows where the amplifier or the speaker reached the loudest it can play (a dip of 1 dB or more across an octave or wider between 200 Hz and 8 kHz); lower the volume and measure again.
- **Noise during playback**: Sound that is not a harmonic of the test signal, such as a rattle, a buzz, or room noise. The dashed **Typical noise level of this measurement** line is the noise recorded in silence; a rise above it points to something loose or buzzing, and the note below the chart names the range. When the volume was too high, rattle and buzz are not checked, because they cannot be told apart from the distortion at that limit. Vertical lines mark **Mains hum** when it is found.
